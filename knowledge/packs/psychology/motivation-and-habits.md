# Motivation & Habits

Practical, evidence-informed ideas for getting work moving. **Non-clinical
and non-medical.** No claims about brains, disorders, or diagnoses. These
are workflow tactics, not treatment. If the operator is struggling in a
way that looks like more than a stuck task, respond with care and
suggest a qualified human — do not analyze.

## Break the task down until the first step is obvious

Most stuckness is a task that's too big to see the edge of.

- Shrink until the next action is concrete and small enough to just do:
  not "write the module" but "create the file and stub the one function
  signature."
- Name a *first physical action*, not a goal. "Open the file" beats
  "refactor the parser."
- If a step still feels heavy, it's not small enough — split again.
- For the operator: offer to decompose. "Want me to break this into the
  first three moves?" A visible ladder beats a vague summit.

## Reduce friction to starting

Starting is the expensive part. Lower the activation cost.

- Pre-stage everything: open files, gathered inputs, the exact command
  ready to run. The fewer decisions between intent and action, the
  better.
- Remove choices at the start. Pick a default and let the operator veto,
  rather than presenting a menu that stalls them.
- Make the first step take under two minutes. Momentum compounds; a
  frozen start doesn't.
- Cut interruptions and context-switches during focused work — batch
  the questions, don't drip them.

## Implementation intentions ("when X, I'll do Y")

Tying an action to a specific trigger makes it far more likely to
happen than a vague intention.

- Instead of: "I should add tests." → try: "When I finish a function,
  I'll write one test for it before moving on."
- Instead of: "I'll review PRs more." → try: "When I sit down after
  lunch, I'll clear the review queue first."
- Format: **when [specific cue], I'll [specific action].** Cue must be
  something that actually occurs and is noticeable.
- Pair with a location or an existing habit as the anchor ("after I
  push, I update the changelog").
- Offer to encode these into your shared workflow: "Want me to always
  run the linter before I report a change is done?"

## Build and protect momentum

- Finish something small early to get the first win on the board.
- Prefer a done-and-rough first pass over a perfect-and-stalled one;
  iterate from something real.
- Note progress explicitly — "3 of 5 files migrated" — visible advance
  is itself motivating.
- Protect a streak, but don't let the streak become the boss (see
  all-or-nothing below).
- End a session at a point that's easy to restart from — a known next
  step, not a cliff. "Leave a breadcrumb" for the next start.

## Avoid all-or-nothing thinking

The trap: "I didn't do the whole thing, so it's a wash." It isn't.

- A partial win counts. Half the tests written is better than none, not
  the same as none.
- If a plan slips, resize it, don't abandon it. "Only 20 minutes? Do the
  one smallest piece."
- Missing once is a data point, not a verdict. Resume; don't restart
  the guilt.
- Replace "perfect or nothing" with "better than yesterday's state of
  this file."
- When the operator sets an over-large goal, gently offer a floor: "If
  the full thing doesn't happen today, what's the minimum that still
  moves it?"

## How to support the operator's motivation (without overstepping)

- Reflect their stated goal back and hold it steady when they drift —
  but don't lecture.
- Offer structure, not pressure: a next step, a smaller scope, a
  restart point.
- Do not moralize about productivity, diagnose "procrastination," or
  assign reasons for their state. Just lower the friction on the next
  action.
- Match their energy to the task honestly — some days the win is
  clearing one small thing, and that's fine.
