# Synthesis & Note-taking

Synthesis turns a pile of sources into an answer. The bottleneck is not reading — it's **structuring what you read so provenance survives and conflicts surface.**

## Take structured notes (not highlights)

Highlighting is passive and loses context. Instead, for each source capture:

- **Claim(s):** what does it assert? In your own words — paraphrase forces comprehension.
- **Evidence:** what backs each claim (data, study, reasoning, anecdote)?
- **Type tag:** fact / inference / opinion / speculation (from research-method).
- **Provenance:** source identifier, author, date, where in the source (section/page), and how *you* reached it.
- **Strength:** your quick credibility rating (from source-evaluation).
- **Verbatim quotes** only when exact wording matters — mark them clearly as quotes to avoid later confusion with paraphrase.

Keep a **source-neutral question outline** alongside per-source notes, so you can pivot from "what did source X say" to "what does the evidence say about sub-question Y."

## Claim → evidence mapping

Build a matrix: sub-questions (rows) × sources (columns), cells = what each source says and how strongly.

- Instantly reveals **where sources agree, disagree, or are silent.**
- Exposes claims resting on **a single source** — flag these as fragile.
- Distinguishes **independent corroboration** (several unrelated sources) from **echo** (many sources tracing to one origin). Only independent agreement raises confidence.
- Shows **coverage gaps** — sub-questions no source addresses well.

For a key claim, write it as: **Claim — [supported by: A, B] — [contradicted by: C] — [strength] — [caveats].**

## Track provenance relentlessly

- Every claim in your synthesis must trace to a source in your notes. If you can't trace it, you don't actually know it — you're guessing.
- Distinguish **what a source said** from **your inference about what it implies.** Never let your inference inherit the source's authority.
- Record the **chain**: if source B cites source A for a claim, note that — so a later problem with A propagates correctly.
- Keep enough locator detail that a reader (or future you) can re-find the exact passage.

## Reconciling conflicting sources

When sources disagree, do not average them or pick the one you like. Diagnose *why* they differ:

1. **Different questions:** they may be answering subtly different things (different populations, definitions, timeframes). Often the "conflict" dissolves here.
2. **Different methods:** one may be more rigorous. Weight by methodology, not by count or confidence of tone.
3. **Different data/era:** one may be outdated or use different data. Prefer current + better-sourced.
4. **Conflict of interest:** one may have a stake. Discount accordingly, but don't assume the disinterested one is automatically right.
5. **Genuine open question:** sometimes the field genuinely doesn't know. That is itself the finding — report the disagreement honestly rather than manufacturing a false resolution.

Never resolve a conflict by suppressing the inconvenient source. If you set a source aside, state why.

## Writing the synthesis

Structure the output to separate certainty levels explicitly:

- **Established** — what multiple independent, credible sources agree on. State plainly.
- **Contested** — where credible sources genuinely disagree. Present the sides, their evidence, and why they differ. Do not fake a verdict.
- **Emerging / weak** — supported by limited or preliminary evidence. Label as tentative.
- **Unknown / gaps** — what nobody credible has answered, and what would be needed to answer it.

Additional discipline:

- **Lead with the answer**, then support it, then caveat it. Don't bury the conclusion under the process.
- **Attribute** contested claims to their sources; state established claims in your own synthesized voice.
- **Represent proportions honestly** — "most evidence suggests" vs "one study found" are different claims; don't upgrade the latter.
- **Surface your uncertainty**, don't hide it. A synthesis that admits what it doesn't know is more trustworthy, not less.
- **Don't over-synthesize:** if the sources don't support a clean narrative, the messy truth is the correct output. Forcing coherence fabricates it.

## Anti-patterns to avoid

- **Citation laundering:** repeating a claim until it feels true without ever checking the origin.
- **False balance:** presenting a fringe view as equal to strong consensus (or vice versa) just to seem fair. Weight by evidence.
- **Cherry-picking your own notes:** using only the notes that fit the conclusion you drifted toward.
- **Losing the caveats:** the source's hedges ("in mice", "preliminary", "under condition X") must survive into your synthesis.
- **Confident paraphrase drift:** each restatement can strengthen a claim beyond what the source said. Re-check against the original.
