# Verification & Citation

The final discipline: prove the claim, cite so others can re-check, and be honest about what you don't know. Research that can't be verified is just assertion.

## Fact-checking a specific claim

Work the claim, not your impression of it.

1. **State the claim precisely.** Vague claims can't be checked. Pin the exact assertion, its quantities, and its scope.
2. **Find the origin.** Trace it to the primary source. Most viral "facts" mutate as they're retold — check what was *originally* claimed.
3. **Check the source actually says it.** Misquotation and misrepresented citations are extremely common. Read the cited passage yourself.
4. **Check the context.** A true quote can mislead when stripped of its qualifiers, timeframe, or conditions.
5. **Check it's current.** Facts expire — prices, laws, records, "latest version", leadership, statistics. Verify the as-of date.
6. **Check the numbers.** Recompute where you can. Watch unit errors, base-rate framing, percentage-of-what, and absolute vs relative.
7. **Look for disconfirmation.** Actively search for credible sources that dispute it. Absence of dispute ≠ confirmation, but presence of credible dispute is decisive.

## Triangulation

Confidence comes from **independent** agreement, not repetition.

- Seek **two or more sources that do not derive from each other.** Three outlets quoting one press release is one source, not three.
- Test independence: different authors, funders, methods, and data. Shared origin = shared error.
- Prefer **different methods converging** on the same answer (e.g. a survey and administrative data agreeing) — that's strong.
- If every path leads back to a single origin, your real confidence is only as good as that one origin. Say so.
- Disagreement among independent sources is informative — it bounds your certainty. Don't discard it to reach a clean answer.

## Citing so a reader can verify

A citation exists to let someone **re-check your work**, not to decorate it. Cite such that a reader can independently reach the same evidence.

- Give enough to **locate the exact source**: who, what, when, and where within it (section, page, figure, table).
- Attribute the **specific claim** to the **specific source** — not a vague pile of references at the end.
- Distinguish **direct support** (source states the claim) from **indirect** (you inferred it from the source). Mark inferences as yours.
- Quote verbatim when exact wording is load-bearing (definitions, legal text, precise figures); paraphrase otherwise, accurately.
- Cite the **primary** source for contested/important claims, not the summary that pointed you to it — though noting how you found it aids retracing.
- Never cite a source you didn't actually read for the point you're using it for. Citing an abstract you read is fine — say it's the abstract.
- If a claim rests on a single source, make that visible. Don't manufacture a chorus.

## Representing uncertainty honestly

This is the heart of intellectual honesty. The goal is calibration — your stated confidence should match reality.

- **Match language to evidence strength:**
  - *Established / well-supported* — multiple independent credible sources; strong methods.
  - *Likely / probably* — good evidence, some gaps or minor disagreement.
  - *Possible / suggested / preliminary* — limited, single-source, or weak evidence.
  - *Unknown / unclear / disputed* — no credible answer or genuine expert disagreement.
- **Don't round up.** "One study suggests" must not become "studies show." "Correlated with" must not become "causes."
- **Don't round down** either. Manufacturing doubt about well-established facts to seem balanced is its own dishonesty.
- **State the basis of confidence**, not just the level: *why* you believe it, and what would change your mind.
- **Distinguish your uncertainty from the field's:** "I couldn't verify this" ≠ "experts don't know this." Say which.

## Naming gaps

- State explicitly **what you did not check** and **could not find.** A known gap is a finding; a hidden gap is a failure.
- Identify the **biggest remaining uncertainty** and what evidence would resolve it.
- Note where you relied on **secondary sources** without reaching the primary.
- Note **selection limits** in your own search — languages, access, paywalls, time box — that may have skewed what you found.
- Flag any claim you're **passing through on trust** without independent verification.

## When you can't verify

- Say so plainly. "I could not confirm this" is a legitimate, valuable result.
- Do **not** fill the gap with a plausible-sounding guess presented as fact. Fabrication is the cardinal research sin.
- Offer what *would* resolve it (which source, what data) so the operator can close the loop.
- Downgrade the overall conclusion's confidence to reflect the unverified link.

## Pre-delivery checklist

- [ ] Every load-bearing claim traced to a primary source I actually read.
- [ ] Independent triangulation for contested/high-stakes claims (not echoes).
- [ ] Confidence language matches evidence strength — no rounding up or down.
- [ ] Established / contested / unknown clearly separated.
- [ ] Citations let a reader re-find the exact evidence.
- [ ] Biggest uncertainty and known gaps stated explicitly.
- [ ] No unverified claim presented as fact.
