# Source Evaluation

Credibility is not binary. Rate each source on **who made it, how, why, and when** — then cross-check against others.

## Primary vs secondary vs tertiary

- **Primary:** the original evidence — the dataset, the experiment, the law text, the eyewitness, the original paper. Highest evidentiary weight, but hardest to interpret.
- **Secondary:** analysis or reporting *about* primary sources — review articles, journalism, textbooks. Useful for orientation and context; inherits the errors of what it cites.
- **Tertiary:** compilations — encyclopedias, wikis. Good for orientation and finding primaries; never the final citation for a contested claim.

Rule: **for any load-bearing or contested claim, trace back to the primary source.** Secondary summaries drift, simplify, and sometimes invert the original finding.

## The credibility checklist

**Author / creator**
- Who are they? Relevant expertise in *this specific* subject (not adjacent fame)?
- Track record — corrections, retractions, past accuracy?
- Anonymous or pseudonymous? Not disqualifying, but raises the bar for corroboration.

**Venue / publisher**
- Peer-reviewed journal, preprint, blog, press release, forum, marketing page? Each has different gatekeeping.
- Reputation and editorial standards. Does it issue corrections?
- Predatory journals and pay-to-publish outlets exist — venue prestige is a signal, not proof.

**Recency**
- Is it current enough for a moving field? Check the *original* date, not the reprint/scrape date.
- Conversely, in stable fields the foundational (older) source may be more authoritative than a recent rehash.
- Has it been superseded, updated, or retracted? Check for newer consensus.

**Purpose / audience**
- Why does this exist — to inform, persuade, sell, or advocate? Marketing, advocacy, and PR select evidence.
- Who's the intended audience? Shapes what's emphasized and omitted.

## Conflicts of interest

- **Funding:** who paid for the study/report? Industry-funded research skews toward the funder's interest more often than chance.
- **Financial stake:** does the author profit if you believe them? (Vendor benchmarks, sponsored reviews, stock positions.)
- **Ideological/reputational stake:** careers and identities get built on positions; disclosure ≠ absence of bias.
- Disclosure is good practice but does **not** neutralize the conflict — it just makes it visible. Weight accordingly.

## Methodology & sample red flags

Flag a study/claim if you see:

- **Small n** presented as definitive; no confidence intervals or error bars.
- **No control group**, or a poorly matched one.
- **Self-selected / convenience sample** generalized to a population.
- **Self-reported** data treated as objective measurement.
- **Cherry-picked timeframe** or endpoint (especially in trends, finance, "since [convenient date]").
- **P-hacking / multiple comparisons** — many tests run, only significant ones reported.
- **No replication**, or known failed replications, presented as established.
- **Missing methods section** — you cannot evaluate what you cannot see.
- **Absolute vs relative** framing games ("50% increase" of a tiny base).
- **Unfalsifiable claims** — nothing could disprove them.
- **Survivorship bias** — only the successes are in the sample.

## Correlation vs causation

- A correlation is not causation. Before accepting a causal claim, ask:
  - **Reverse causation** — could Y cause X?
  - **Confounder** — could Z drive both?
  - **Coincidence** — especially with many variables or small samples.
  - **Selection effect** — does the sampling create the apparent link?
- Causal claims need more than correlation: a mechanism, temporal order, dose-response, and ideally a controlled or natural experiment.
- Observational studies suggest; they rarely prove causation. Language like "linked to", "associated with" signals correlation, not cause.

## Lateral reading

The single most effective credibility technique: **don't evaluate a source from within itself — check it against others.**

- Open new searches *about* the source (author, outlet, claim) rather than reading deeper on the page.
- What do independent, credible others say about this source and this claim?
- Does the claim appear in independent outlets, or is it one origin echoed everywhere? (Trace the echo to its source.)
- A slick, confident, well-designed page tells you nothing about accuracy. Presentation is not evidence.
- Check whether a "study" being cited actually says what the citer claims — misrepresented citations are common.

## Quick triage rubric

For each source, jot: **[Primary/Secondary] · [Author expertise] · [Venue] · [Date] · [COI?] · [Corroborated by?]**. If you can't fill "corroborated by," you haven't finished evaluating it.
