# Argument Analysis

How to take an argument apart and judge whether it actually earns its conclusion. The goal is to separate *what is claimed* from *what is shown*.

## Anatomy: premises → conclusion
An **argument** offers reasons (**premises**) in support of a **conclusion**. To analyze one:
1. **Find the conclusion.** What is this trying to get me to accept? (Signal words: "therefore," "so," "which means.")
2. **List the premises.** What reasons are given? (Signal words: "because," "since," "given that.")
3. **Surface hidden premises.** Most arguments rely on unstated assumptions. Make them explicit — the weak link usually hides there.
4. **Check the link.** Do the premises, if true, actually support the conclusion?

## Validity vs. soundness — keep them separate
- **Valid:** the conclusion *follows logically* from the premises. If the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be true. Validity is about structure, not truth.
- **Sound:** the argument is valid **and** its premises are actually true.
- A valid argument can be false: "All birds can fly; penguins are birds; therefore penguins fly" is valid but unsound (premise 1 is false).
- **So attack on two fronts:** (a) Is the logic valid? (b) Are the premises actually true? An argument fails if *either* breaks.

## Separate claims from evidence
- A **claim** is an assertion; **evidence** is what would make it credible. "X is true" is not evidence for X.
- Ask of each claim: "What would show this, and has any of it been offered?"
- Distinguish **descriptive** ("what is") from **normative** ("what ought") claims — evidence bears on the first; values and goals bear on the second. Smuggling an "ought" out of an "is" is a common move.
- Watch for **unfalsifiable** claims — if no possible observation could contradict it, it isn't telling you about the world.

## Common fallacies (defects in the reasoning)
- **Straw man:** refuting a distorted, weaker version of the opponent's position instead of their real one. *Fix: quote the strongest actual version.*
- **False dilemma (false dichotomy):** presenting two options as exhaustive when more exist. *Fix: ask "are these really the only choices?"*
- **Ad hominem:** attacking the person rather than their argument. *Fix: the argument stands or falls on its own merits regardless of who makes it.*
- **Slippery slope:** claiming one step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome without showing the mechanism or probability of each link. *Fix: demand the causal chain and its likelihood.*
- **Appeal to authority:** "an expert/leader said so, therefore true." Expertise raises the prior but doesn't settle it; authorities disagree and err. *Fix: ask for the underlying argument, and whether it's in their actual domain.*
- **Appeal to nature:** "it's natural, therefore good" (or "unnatural, therefore bad"). Natural and good are different properties. *Fix: name the actual harm or benefit.*
- **Circular reasoning (begging the question):** the conclusion is smuggled into the premises. *Fix: check whether a premise just restates the conclusion.*
- **Hasty generalization:** broad claim from too few or unrepresentative cases. *Fix: check sample size and selection.*
- **Post hoc:** "B followed A, so A caused B." *Fix: correlation ≠ causation; look for a mechanism and alternatives.*
- **Appeal to emotion:** substituting fear, pity, or outrage for reasons. *Fix: ask what the argument is once the feeling is set aside.*
- **Tu quoque / whataboutism:** deflecting a claim by pointing at the accuser's own faults. *Fix: their hypocrisy doesn't make the original claim false.*

A fallacy means the argument is *unsupported*, not that the conclusion is *false* — a bad argument can still reach a true conclusion by luck. Reject the reasoning, stay open on the claim.

## Steelmanning
Before you critique a position, restate it in its **strongest, most charitable** form — stronger than its own proponent put it. Then engage *that*.

- **Why:** If you can only beat the weak version (straw man), you've learned nothing and persuaded no one. Beating the strong version is the only critique that counts.
- **Test:** Could the other side say "yes, that's exactly what I mean, only better"? If not, you haven't steelmanned yet.
- Steelmanning also protects *you*: it's the fastest way to find the real flaw, or to discover you were wrong.

## A working checklist
1. What exactly is the **conclusion**?
2. What are the **premises**, stated and hidden?
3. Is it **valid** (does it follow)?
4. Are the premises **true** (is it sound)?
5. Any **fallacies** in the link?
6. Have I engaged the **steelman**, not a straw man?
7. What's the **strongest counter-argument**, and does it survive?
